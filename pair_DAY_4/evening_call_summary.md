When Ephrata went through my score_dimension() explainer, the part that landed was the distinction between systematic inflation and random noise. She said she had assumed that if the scorer was wrong sometimes it would average out across the benchmark — I had made the same assumption before working through it. The mechanism that changes the picture is that the fall-through always awards 1.0, never 0.0. It's a one-directional ceiling, not a coin flip. Every unrecognized key pulls the composite score up, never down. Once she saw that framing — the failure mode has no opposing pressure — the corruption became obvious. What she pushed back on was the fix section. I had written that the guard should raise ValueError on any unrecognized key, but she asked: does raising an error at scoring time make sense, or does it make more sense to catch the bad key before the evaluation run starts? That's the right question. A scorer that crashes mid-benchmark is worse than a scorer that fails silently if you have thousands of examples queued. I revised the fix to separate the two: a validation pass at load time that checks every key in the task JSONL against the registered scorer set before any example is evaluated, and a ValueError guard inside score_dimension() as a secondary defense for keys that slip through. The load-time check is the one that matters for preventing silent corruption at scale.

Going through Ephrata's IRR explainer, the intra/inter distinction was the part she said she hadn't had a clean mental model for before. Her original framing was a single "judge reliability" concept — I showed her why those two need to be diagnosed separately, because a judge can be perfectly stable and consistently wrong if its biases are systematic. The QWK threshold landed clearly: she hadn't known which metric to use for ordinal scores, and having a specific number (≥ 0.80 for selection decisions) gave her a target she could actually test against. What she pushed back on was the claim that position bias is one-directional. She asked: how do you know the judge always favors the last position rather than just being noisy about position? I pointed to the structural reason — the judge's training data has more text where the final speaker's contribution is framed as the resolution — but she was right that I had stated it as fact without flagging that the direction can vary by model architecture and training corpus. I added a caveat: run a position-swap test on 20 pairs before assuming the direction, because a recency-biased judge and a primacy-biased judge require different controls. She revised her grounding commit to include the position-swap as step one of the validation protocol.
